What this brain can do

  • Learned the rules from the games it played. How a stone falls and what four in a row is were never given.
  • Moving first, it beat the perfect solver in 40 of 40 games, every move perfect, on this page in a browser, and the solver at 90% in 20 of 20. Moving second, where the first player wins with perfect play, it beat the solver at 70% in 29 of 30 games and drew the other.
  • Beats AlphaZero, both the model trained with alpha-zero-general's published recipe and a longer run of 120 iterations, at 100 simulations per move: every game, first and second.
  • Reads the threats standing on a board off its own records: a window one cell short of a line, and whether that cell can be played at once. Two of its own it can reach decide a position; one of the opponent's decides it the other way.
  • Remembers. Every position its searches proved stays proven, and on every board it has seen it remembers the columns the winning side played, its own and the opponent's. Schooled against the perfect solver, that memory carries the solver's opening play into its own.
  • Imagines the boards its moves lead to and searches them as deep as 131,072 imagined boards reach, 524,288 once eighteen stones are down: ten plies in the middle game, twenty and more near the end, where a proof is close. A validator rejects an imagined board that is not the old board plus one stone.
  • Remembers what it proves while you play. A position its search proves stays proven for the rest of your visit. What the life and the school wrote stays as it was loaded: in a battery of games in the browser, letting a visitor's games rewrite the records turned three won positions into losses and draws.
  • Small. Two record cortices of 2,000 cells and an agent of 187 neurons; the life trained in under an hour on one CPU core.

What plays here

The Connect Four brain of cadence-examples, seed 10 of the five-seed acceptance run of 16 September 2026, with its search set deeper than it trained at and the memory of its schooling installed. Two record cortices and a search: the drop records hold where a stone lands in each column, the line records hold which windows of four cells are finished lines and how the games that held each window pattern ended, and negamax with alpha-beta pruning searches the boards the records imagine. The threats the search reads are read off the line records' complete field; the rule that turns them into a value, like the search itself, is supplied. The small settled regions of the agent carry the event transaction: sensory and goal neurons drive a workspace of 16, the workspace couples to a dynamics region of 8, the motor neurons read the column off the workspace, and the context neurons carry a trace of the last workspace state. The scan draws every settling step of every phase as it happens, and the synapses flash when an update moves them. The rules were never given: the world emits boards and the brain writes what it observes.

How it was trained

One life of 1,200 games. 200 random games first. Then 800 games against a mixture: 20% a random opponent, 20% an opponent that takes a win and blocks one, 20% frozen snapshots of itself, 20% Pascal Pons' perfect solver playing the perfect column 30% of the time and a random one otherwise, and 20% the solver at 70%. Then 200 games against 20% the one-ply opponent, 40% the solver at 70% and 40% the solver at 100%. The solver is an opponent and nothing else: the brain sees its moves and the outcomes of the games, never its scores. Perfect play alone would make every game a loss and every value zero, which is why the strengths are graded.

Every move either side makes is written into the records that read it: the column readings take the row the stone occupied, the windows of the new board take incomplete, and when the move won, the window through the new stone takes complete. When the game ends, every window pattern the game held takes the outcome of the side that had just moved, and every board of the game, as its mover saw it, takes the column the winning side played on it. The writes touch only the cells the reading lit, so one game is written almost exactly and the games before it stay.

Then it went to school. The value the records read is flat in the opening: the seven first moves lie within 0.003 of each other, and the search at ten plies cannot see a loss thirty plies away. So the brain watched the perfect solver play itself, both sides perfect or one of them at 90%, 70% or 50%, 455,000 games in all, and remembered the winning side's columns on every board; where its memory already held a column for a board, the watched game followed it, so every game extended the brain's own lines. It also played the solver itself, first and second, remembering every position its searches proved. The page carries 1,007,760 boards with the columns winners played and 72,380 proven positions: every board seen in two watched games or more, and the rest by fewest stones. When the search proves nothing, the brain plays the column winners played most on the board in front of it, unless the search saw that column lose.

The trained life searched four plies within 1,024 imagined transitions per move. The page plays the same records with a search that deepens until 131,072 imagined transitions are spent, or 524,288 once eighteen stones are down. An earlier version also stopped at sixteen plies, and in a browser battery that cap threw a won endgame whose proof sat two plies further, reachable in a tenth of a second. The search is supplied and the records are what was learned, so the setting changes no learned number. In headless Chromium on a 2024 laptop the browser answers in 0.48 s a move on average and within 3.1 s on the slowest move, a deep proof near the end; the memory, 20 MB and 6.5 MB compressed, loads before the first move.

Results

The paired protocol: 100 games per opponent, the brain opening half of them; win 1, draw 0.5, loss 0, with the score split by who opened. Every move the brain made was scored by the perfect solver: a blunder is a move that turns a won position into a drawn or lost one, or a drawn one into a lost one, counted over the positions that were not already lost, and an optimal move keeps the solver's best score. The brain played as this page plays it: ten plies within 131,072 imagined transitions, sixteen within 1,048,576 from eighteen stones on, with its memory.

Wins, draws and losses of games played on this page in headless Chromium: the opponent's columns were clicked on the board with the mouse, the brain answered in the page's own JavaScript, and every move the brain made was graded afterwards by the perfect solver. The solver at a strength plays the perfect column with that probability and a random one otherwise, ties among perfect columns drawn at random; AlphaZero samples its first three stones from its visit counts, so the games differ.

Looking ahead: on 200 trap positions whose only sound column is refuted between four and more than ten plies later, drawn from random play and graded by the solver, the brain keeps the theoretical result in 89.5% of them at eight plies and 87.5% at four; minimax at 1,024 nodes keeps it in 57.0%, the one-ply opponent in 50.5%.

For scale: minimax at 1,024 nodes blunders in 40% to 50% of its positions against the same opponents and scores 0.375 and 0.260 against the published-recipe AlphaZero at 25 and 100 simulations. The first player wins Connect Four with perfect play, so against the full solver a paired score of 0.5 is perfect play: every game opened by the solver is lost by everything, and the games the brain opens are the ones it can win.

AlphaZero here is alpha-zero-general's Connect Four, trained by us in PyTorch on one A10G, in two runs. The published recipe: 100 self-play games and 25 MCTS simulations per move per iteration, 32 iterations, the model of iteration 24 (the 14th accepted network, sha256 a7477699); it scores 0.738 against the one-ply opponent, 0.688 against minimax at 1,024 nodes and 0.562 against the solver at 70%. The longer run: the same recipe at 50 simulations per move for 120 iterations, its best network when the bench ran (the 33rd accepted, at iteration 106, sha256 873e1660). In the paired games AlphaZero's openings are sampled from its visit counts for the first three stones so the games differ, and its tree starts fresh each game.

What it cannot do

Win as the second player against perfect play: the first player wins Connect Four, and the brain loses every game it plays second against the full solver, as everything does. Nor does it play perfectly off the lines it remembers. Against a first player who plays the perfect column seven times in ten it threw a won position in 5 of 30 games, and nine times in ten in 3 of 60, between the 13th and 21st stone, in positions where one column wins and every other loses and its records read the columns within a few hundredths of each other; it still won 29 of the 30 and drew the other. Schooling on 260,000 more games with an imperfect opponent raised how much of those lines its memory covers by a few points past the thirteenth stone: watched games reach the positions an imperfect opponent opens too rarely for memory to close this. The rule that turns the threats it reads into a value is supplied, like the search; a version that learns that rule is the next lane.
