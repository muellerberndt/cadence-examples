# The real fly: a literature brief on the free behaviour of adult Drosophila melanogaster

Purpose. A simulated fly in a 4 by 3 by 2.6 m room produces an ethogram. This brief collects the numbers that the real animal produces, so that the simulated ethogram can be measured against them. Every number is tied to a primary source (author, year, journal) with the species, conditions and sample size the source gives. Where a number could not be found in a primary source, the brief says so. Derived quantities (for example a mean rate computed from two reported numbers) are labelled as derived.

Scope. All numbers are for adult D. melanogaster unless a row says otherwise. Almost all of them come from laboratory arenas that are much smaller than a room: wind tunnels of 1.5 by 0.3 by 0.3 m, cylinders of 1 to 2 m diameter, and walking arenas of 5 to 30 cm. One field study (Leitch 2021) gives outdoor flight speeds. No study measured a fly's full-day ethogram in a room-sized space, so the closing table combines laboratory kinematics with tube-based time budgets.

Compiled 2026-09-24 from web-accessible full texts and abstracts.

## 1. Free flight kinematics

Flies fly in straight segments separated by rapid body saccades. Saccades are banked turns. Speed is regulated visually around a preferred ground speed and rises when the visual world has low contrast or when the fly is outdoors.

## 3. Odour plume tracking in flight and on foot

Flying flies use a cast-and-surge strategy with fixed sensory-motor delays. Walking flies turn upwind and speed up on odour onset and increase turning after odour loss; in a turbulent plume they turn in stereotyped saccades whose direction is biased by the timing of recent odour encounters.

## 4. Landing and takeoff

Escape takeoffs are prepared by leg repositioning about 200 ms before the jump, and the jump itself comes in a fast wings-down mode and a slower wings-up mode. Voluntary takeoffs raise the wings first and leave the ground more slowly and more stably. Landing is a three-module sequence: turn toward the target, decelerate on retinal expansion, extend the legs at a fixed retinal size.

|---|---|---|

## 5. Feeding

Flies eat in discrete meals whose volume and frequency are controlled separately. Within a meal, feeding is organised into bursts of sips. Hunger shortens the interval between bursts and lengthens bursts. Dethier's The Hungry Fly (1976, Harvard University Press) is a study of the blowfly Phormia regina; its proboscis-extension thresholds are not Drosophila numbers and are not used here.

## 6. Grooming

Grooming follows a body-part sequence from front to back, produced by a suppression hierarchy. Bouts last 0.15 to 2 s, legs sweep at about 6 Hz, and alternation between head cleaning and front-leg rubbing runs at about 0.4 to 0.5 Hz. Most grooming data come from dust-coated flies; spontaneous grooming rates come from the 24 h tube budgets.

## 7. Walking

Walking speed has a mode near 15 to 18 mm/s with a second mode at zero. Flies spend most of the time near walls in small arenas, and walk-stop switching happens on a timescale of seconds. Phototaxis is a decision that depends on flight ability.

## 8. Learning

Olfactory conditioning works on groups of about 100 flies and produces strong, lasting memories. Place learning in a heated arena and operant heading learning in a flight simulator work on single flies.

## Reference ethogram: ten numbers for a simulated fly in a 4 by 3 by 2.6 m room

The room is far larger than any laboratory arena in this brief. The laboratory numbers therefore bound the simulation: saccade rate should fall toward the spontaneous baseline away from walls, cruise speed should sit between the indoor and outdoor values, and the daily budget should follow the tube studies because they are the only full-day records.
